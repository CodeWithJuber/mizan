# Can the Arabic/Quranic root-and-pattern space be a universal meaning space (interlingua) for Hinglish/Roman Urdu, Urdu, Hindi, English and Arabic in a small CPU-only model?

Research notes, 29 October 2026. Scope: linguistic coverage, prior art on language-neutral meaning spaces, Arabic/Urdu/Hindi morphology and transliteration resources, how to build the mapping on 2 vCPU / 2 GB with the teacher asked once, and an evaluation plan with expected numbers. These notes extend the 28 Oct 2026 report (`/research/reports/MIZAN self learning architecture.md`, sections "Tamil arrives through Lughah…", D2 and D9) and build on the sibling measurements in `experiments_cpu_and_root_coverage.md` (E1 CPU throughput, E2 Quranic-root coverage) and `audit_ruh_model_state.md` in this folder. Everything in "Inferences" is my own reasoning; everything in "Cited Findings" has a source.

One-line verdict up front (inference from the findings below): the Quranic/Arabic root inventory can be a **strong partial channel** (religious, abstract, legal, scholarly and emotional vocabulary across Urdu, Hindi-Urdu colloquial speech, Hinglish and Arabic), but it cannot be *the* universal meaning space: a third of everyday Urdu tokens are provably non-Arabic, roughly 99% of Urdu verbs are Indo-Aryan, Hinglish is majority-English by token, and even modern Arabic has fewer than half of its running word forms attested in the Quran. The buildable design is a **factored representation**: `(concept_id, root_id, pattern_id, bytes)` where `root_id` is non-null only for the share of words a verified lexicon covers, `concept_id` is a learned cross-lingual slot seeded from open concept lists (Concepticon, Arabic WordNet 4.0, IndoWordNet) and the teacher's one-time proposals, and the byte channel carries everything else.

## Key Question 1 — Linguistics: how much of Urdu, Hindi and Hinglish maps to Arabic roots, and what is the honest coverage ceiling?

### Takeaway
By type, Arabic + Persian make up roughly half of Urdu's basic vocabulary (Arabic 29.9%, Persian 21.7% in the only corpus-based count found, 1,000 basic words) and 25–30% of its dictionary; by token in everyday text the Arabic-root share is far lower because function words and nearly all verbs are Indo-Aryan, and the sibling measurement E2 found 36.6% of everyday Urdu tokens contain letters Arabic does not have. Hinglish chat is majority English by token (LinCE: 84,752 English vs 29,958 Hindi tokens of 146,722 in the largest Hindi-English set). No token-frequency study of Arabic-origin words in running Urdu, Hindi or Hinglish was found; that number must be measured by MIZAN itself.

### Cited Findings

**Urdu vocabulary by origin (type-level counts)**

**Arabic root inventory and Quranic subset**
- Classical Arabic lexicon size: Tāj al-ʿArūs embraces "about 12.000 roots"; a CA-vs-MSA dictionary intersection found **7,600 triliteral roots in the classical dictionary, of which 4,143 (54.51%) do not recur in the MSA dictionary and 3,396 (44.68%) do**; of the 4,143 archaic roots 2,626 (63%) were productive — [Hafeedh & Laskar, "Triliteral Roots and their Transition from Classical Arabic to MSA", JALT (2024)](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/jalt/wp-content/uploads/sites/1121/2024/02/HafeedhLaskarJALT213.pdf)
- Other published counts (surfaced by search; the page itself returned HTTP 403 to the fetcher, so these are as summarised in the search result): Lisān al-ʿArab ~9,273 roots in total (6,723 triliteral + 2,516 quadriliteral); Hans Wehr 2,967 triliteral + 362 quadriliteral; Maqāyīs al-Lugha 4,963 roots; Hegazi & El-Sharkawi: Lisān al-ʿArab 6,350 triliteral and 2,500 quadriliteral roots "although only 1,200 of these are still used in Modern Arabic"; Ryding: 5,000–6,500 roots in current use; theoretical maximum 21,952 — [arabic-for-nerds.com, "How many roots does Arabic have?"](https://arabic-for-nerds.com/grammar/how-many-roots-does-arabic-have/)

**Semitic root sharing and "roots as semantic primes"**
- Zammit (1998) compared the Qur'anic Arabic root inventory with nine Semitic languages (Arabic, Ge'ez, Epigraphic South Arabian, Syriac, Aramaic, Hebrew, Phoenician, Ugaritic, Akkadian) as a "mass comparison" of root-morpheme distribution and "confirms the high degree of lexical exclusivity in Arabic", placing it "almost equidistant between North-West Semitic and South Semitic" — [OAR@UM record](https://www.um.edu.mt/library/oar/handle/123456789/101263) (full text restricted; no percentages available)
- Kogan: "ca. 70% of coincidences between Ugaritic and Hebrew on the basic vocabulary", but most of it is "trivial retentions from Proto-Semitic" of "little value for genealogical classification" — [Sefarad article (CSIC)](https://sefarad.revistas.csic.es/index.php/sefarad/article/download/608/705/1215); a student abstract reports MSA–Hebrew "159 cognates out of 203 = 78.33%" on a 203-item list — [Alaslani, CIEL8 abstract](https://brainevo.sitehost.iu.edu/CIEL8/abstracts/Alaslani_Khadeejah_v2.html) (low authority; not peer-reviewed)
- Ibn Fāris's *Maqāyīs al-Lugha* aimed "to establish the basic meanings of word-roots, and the semantic connections between their derivatives"; a 2024 study counts, of 4,659 entries, "approximately 2175 articles have one common meaning, 355 articles have two common meanings, 68 articles have three common meanings and 15 articles have four common meanings" (the shared meaning is called *aṣl* or *miqyās*) — [Wikipedia: Ibn Faris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Faris); [IASJ 2024 study (PDF)](https://iasj.rdd.edu.iq/journals/uploads/2024/12/07/aebabb7d009b4e5b1aa56aa0499e93a7.pdf)
- Bohas's Theory of Matrices and Etymons holds that "the traditional tri-consonant root framework is not sufficient", positing sub-morphemic phonetic-feature *matrices* carrying a "notional invariant" realised by *etymons*; reviewers note it "so far cannot accommodate all the data" — [Mahfoudhi, JAIS 2017](https://journals.uio.no/JAIS/article/view/4583); [Michalski review, Folia Orientalia 52 (2015)](https://journals.pan.pl//Content/109640/PDF/FoliaOrientalia%2052-15%2031%20Michalski-Review.pdf)
- Natural Semantic Metalanguage: the prime set grew from 14 (1972) to 60 (2002) to the current **65** primes (I, you, someone, people, something, body; kind, part; this, the same, other; one, two, some, all, much, little; good, bad; big, small; think, know, want, don't want, feel, see, hear; say, words, true; do, happen, move; be somewhere, there is, be someone/something, is mine; live, die; when, now, before, after, a long time, a short time, for some time, moment; where, here, above, below, far, near, side, inside, touch; not, maybe, can, because, if; very, more; like); "Dozens of languages, including representatives of 16 language groups, have been studied" — [Wikipedia: Natural semantic metalanguage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_semantic_metalanguage)

### Inferences
- **Type vs token.** The 29.9% Arabic / 21.7% Persian figures are shares of a 1,000-word *basic vocabulary list*, i.e. by type. Running text is dominated by function words, pronouns, light verbs (karna, hona, dena) and auxiliaries, all Indo-Aryan (99% of verbs). Combining Dehlavi's 25–30% dictionary share, E2's 36.6% provably-non-Arabic tokens and the verb statistic, I estimate that **15–30% of everyday Urdu tokens carry an Arabic root** (higher in religious or formal text, lower in chat); E2's 10.5% exact Quranic forms is the floor and E2's 63.4% non-excluded tokens the hard ceiling. This must be measured, not assumed: run the Sarf analyzer (`backend/tadbir/sarf.py`) over Lughah `ur.tsv`, the Dakshina Urdu Wikipedia sentences and MASSIVE ur-PK, and publish the three numbers.
- **Hinglish ceiling is much lower.** In LinCE's largest set Hindi tokens are 20.4% of all tokens (29,958/146,722) and English 57.8%. If 15–30% of those Hindi tokens are Arabic-origin, the Arabic-root channel touches about **3–6% of Hinglish chat tokens**, concentrated in exactly the words MIZAN's users care about (dua, namaz, waqt, ilm, sabr, hukm, zaroorat, matlab, khabar, kitab). English carries most Hinglish tokens, so the concept channel for Hinglish must be built from English/Hindi, not from roots.
- **Which Urdu/Hindi words map cleanly.** Words that map to a single triliteral root with the Quranic sense intact are the Arabic nominal borrowings in -at/-ah, maṣdars and participles: ʿilm (ع-ل-م), kitāb (ك-ت-ب), waqt (و-ق-ت), ḥukm (ح-ك-م), fikr (ف-ك-ر), ṣabr (ص-ب-ر), ḥaqq, khabar, ʿadl, īmān, niyyat, ḥāl, maʿlūm, zarūrat (ض-ر-ر), mumkin, mushkil, mazhab/madhhab. Three failure classes break the clean mapping: (i) Indo-Aryan core (pānī, ghar, dekhnā, khānā, bachcha, acchā, bahut) — no root; (ii) Persian intermediaries that are Persian, not Arabic (dil, zindagī, dost, roz, shahr, kam, zyāda, bā-, be-) — a Persian-origin share (21.7% of basic vocabulary) that a Quranic root space silently drops; (iii) semantic drift of Arabic words in Urdu ("different pronunciations and nuances of meaning"), e.g. Urdu *mazdūr* (worker, from مَعْذُور "excused"), *gharīb* (poor, from غَرِيب "stranger"), *mulāqāt* (meeting), so root identity does not guarantee sense identity; the pattern slot and a per-language sense id must disambiguate.
- **Roots are not semantic primes.** Ibn Fāris's own count (fewer than half of 4,659 root entries reduce to a single *aṣl*; 438 have two to four) and Bohas's dissatisfaction with the root as basic unit mean a root is at best a *family* of related concepts, not a prime. The ~1,650 Quranic roots are also an order of magnitude more numerous than NSM's 65 primes and an order of magnitude fewer than the ~6,000 roots in modern Arabic use. The usable analogy is weaker and more honest: the root is a **shared etymon index** that aligns cognate vocabulary across Arabic, Urdu, Hindi-Urdu colloquial speech, Persian-mediated loans and (via Zammit-style comparison) other Semitic languages; concepts must be learned on top of it.
- **What the byte/character channel must carry:** all function words and inflection in every language; all verbs in Urdu/Hindi/Hinglish (≈99% Indo-Aryan); Persian and English loans; named entities; numbers; Roman-Urdu spelling variation (see KQ3); and the Arabic words whose Urdu sense drifted. In Arabic itself the root channel can cover most content words only if a full analyzer (CAMeL r13, not the 1,651-root Quranic table) supplies roots; the Quranic table alone covers 43% of modern Arabic tokens by exact form (E2).

### Gaps
- No corpus study giving the **token-frequency** share of Arabic- or Persian-origin words in running Urdu, Hindi or Hinglish was found; only type-level counts (Maldonado García 2018 via İşsever 2025; Dehlavi) and register-specific work (Bollywood lyrics). The 15–30% Urdu and 3–6% Hinglish figures above are my inference.
- Maldonado García's Persian-and-Arabic paper itself (Pakistan Vision 16(1), 2015) was not retrievable; the percentages are reported second-hand.
- No Arabic–Hebrew–Amharic shared-root percentage from a peer-reviewed lexicostatistical source was found (Kogan 2015, *Genealogical Classification of Semitic*, would have it; not accessible). Zammit's per-language figures are behind restricted access.
- No scholarship was found that treats Semitic roots as cross-linguistic *semantic primes* in the NSM sense; the nearest are Ibn Fāris (one *aṣl* per root, partially) and Bohas (sub-root matrices), both Arabic-internal.

## Key Question 2 — Prior art on language-neutral meaning spaces: what worked, what failed, and what is different now?

### Takeaway
Hand-built interlinguas (KANT, UNL) worked only inside controlled, narrow domains because an interlingua "requires complete resolution of all ambiguities"; learned spaces succeeded where there was massive parallel data (Google's multilingual NMT in 2016, SONAR's 200-language sentence space in 2023, Meta's LCM in Dec 2024 at 1.6–7B parameters). Unsupervised word-embedding alignment fails outright on morphologically rich or distant pairs (P@1 0.00 on en-et, 0.09 on en-fi) but a weak seed (identical strings, numerals, a 25-pair or 5k dictionary) restores 28–45%. What is different for MIZAN: a strong teacher can supply the seed lexicon once, MIZAN already has verifiers, and the interlingua need not be complete because the byte channel carries the remainder.

### Cited Findings

**Interlingua MT history**
- Hutchins: translation among n languages needs n(n−1) direct systems but "just 2n interlingual programs"; "The interlingua approach necessarily requires complete resolution of all ambiguities in the SL text, whereas transfer systems only handle ambiguities inherent in the source language"; ALPAC (1966) was read as "the 'failure' of MT" though it was "the failure of the excessively ambitious approaches" — [Hutchins, HEL 2001 (PDF)](https://aclanthology.org/www.mt-archive.info/00/HEL-2001-Hutchins.pdf); [Hutchins 1990 retrospective](https://aclanthology.org/www.mt-archive.info/90/T&T-1990-Hutchins.pdf)
- Wikipedia's survey: "the definition of an interlingua is difficult and maybe even impossible for a wider domain"; knowledge-based systems cannot build knowledge bases beyond very specific domains; the ideal setting is "multilingual machine translation in a very specific domain"; historical systems DLT (Utrecht, modified Esperanto, 1980s), Fujitsu, Stanford (Schank, Wilks), Grenoble, METAL — [Wikipedia: Interlingual machine translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingual_machine_translation)
- KANT (CMU, for Caterpillar technical documentation) produced an interlingua expression per sentence and credited "a controlled source language and a well-defined application domain" for high accuracy; limiting lexical ambiguity was "the most useful single way to improve accuracy"; ambiguous (disjunctive) interlingua representations still caused target errors; its diagnostic checker "can correctly identify the problem in 90.2% of the cases"; anaphora resolution 89.6%/87.5% — [Mitamura, AMTA 1994](https://aclanthology.org/www.mt-archive.info/AMTA-1994-Mitamura.pdf); [Nyberg, CLAW 1996](https://aclanthology.org/www.mt-archive.info/90/CLAW-1996-Nyberg.pdf); [Nyberg, COLING 1994](https://aclanthology.org/www.mt-archive.info/90/Coling-1994-Nyberg.pdf)
- UNL (UNU/IAS Tokyo, 1996; UNDL Foundation 2001): Universal Words such as `sky(icl>natural world)`, "currently 46 relations", attributes like `@past`; the Wikipedia article gives no adoption figures and is flagged for promotional tone and unclear sources — [Wikipedia: Universal Networking Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Networking_Language)
- Google's multilingual NMT (2016): zero-shot "implicit bridging between language pairs never seen explicitly during training" and "analyses that hints at a universal interlingua representation" — [Johnson et al., arXiv 1611.04558](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.04558)

**Sentence-level concept spaces (SONAR, LCM)**
- SONAR (Aug 2023): "a 24 layers Transformer encoder and a 24 layers Transformer decoder" with a single fixed-size vector bottleneck, initialised from the NLLB 1B dense model, mean pooling, 200 languages, objective L = L_MT + α·L_MSE + β·L_DAE (MSE weight 0.1, DAE weight 0.01); FLORES-200 X→eng xsim error 1.4 (LaBSE 10.7, LASER3 5.1), xsim++ 15.2 (36.1, 36.4) — [Duquenne, Schwenk, Sagot, arXiv 2308.11466](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.11466); embeddings are 1024-d and the released model is **CC-BY-NC 4.0** — [facebook/SONAR model card](https://huggingface.co/facebook/SONAR)
- Large Concept Models (Dec 2024, Meta FAIR): "a concept would often correspond to a sentence"; 1.6B models trained on ~1.3T tokens, one 7B model on ~2.7T tokens; Two-Tower-7B-IT ROUGE-L 36.47 on CNN/DailyMail and 23.71 on XSum vs Llama-3.1-8B-IT 34.97/20.35, Mistral-7B-v0.3-IT 36.06/21.22, Gemma-7B-IT 31.14/18.20; XLSum zero-shot over 42 of 45 languages (3 excluded because SONAR lacks them): English 23.5 vs 20.7 for Llama-3.1-8B-IT, average over the six languages both support (en, fr, hi, pt, es, th) 20.2 vs 19.7; limitations: SONAR texts "tend to be fragile, highlighting a distribution mismatch", SONAR was trained on "bitext machine translation data containing rather short sentences", "The choice of granularity for LCM is not trivial", quantised SONAR reaches only "around 70% of the auto-encoding BLEU score achieved with continuous SONAR embeddings" — [arXiv 2412.08821v2 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.08821v2); follow-up SONAR-LLM trains a token-level cross-entropy through the frozen SONAR decoder — [arXiv 2508.05305](https://arxiv.deeppaper.ai/papers/2508.05305v1)

**Cross-lingual word-embedding alignment**
- MUSE (Conneau et al. 2018), fastText Wikipedia 300-d, 200k most frequent words, supervised Procrustes trained on 5,000 seed pairs: P@1 (Procrustes-CSLS) en-es 81.4, en-ru 51.7, ru-en 63.7, en-zh 42.7, zh-en 36.7, en-eo 29.3; unsupervised adversarial+refine+CSLS en-es 81.7, en-ru 44.0, en-zh 32.5, en-eo 28.2 — [arXiv 1710.04087](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1710.04087)
- Søgaard, Ruder & Vulić (ACL 2018): MUSE unsupervised P@1×100 en-es 81.89, en-hu 45.06, en-pl 46.83, en-tr 32.71, **en-et 00.00, en-fi 00.09, en-el 00.07**; weak supervision from identically spelled strings gives en-et 31.45, en-fi 28.01, en-el 42.96, en-tr 39.22; mixing skip-gram with CBOW spaces drops en-es from 81.89 to 00.00; different-domain corpora drop en-es "close to zero"; "unsupervised bilingual dictionary induction performs much worse on morphologically rich languages that are not dependent marking"; graph (eigenvector) similarity correlates ρ≈0.89 with BDI performance — [arXiv 1805.03620](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1805.03620)
- VecMap (Artetxe, Labaka, Agirre, ACL 2018): fully unsupervised with robust self-learning, 10/10 successful runs: EN-IT 48.13, EN-DE 48.19, **EN-FI 32.63**, EN-ES 37.33 (average accuracy); Conneau's code on the same data: EN-FI best 1.62, 0 of 10 runs successful; supervised 5k-dictionary baselines reach EN-FI 32.94 (Artetxe 2018a); a **25-pair seed** gives 37.27/39.60/28.16 (IT/DE/FI) and a **numerals-only seed** 39.40/40.27/26.47 — [arXiv 1805.06297](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1805.06297)

### Inferences
- **Why interlinguas lost.** Hutchins' "complete resolution of all ambiguities" is the structural reason: an interlingua is a *bottleneck*, so every unresolved ambiguity becomes an error, whereas transfer and later statistical/neural systems could leave ambiguity latent. KANT succeeded by shrinking the input (controlled language, one domain). SONAR/LCM re-created the bottleneck (a 1024-d sentence vector) but learned it from hundreds of millions of parallel sentences with a ~1B-parameter encoder, and still reports fragility, short-sentence bias and non-trivial granularity. Neither path is open to MIZAN: it has no controlled language and no GPU.
- **What is genuinely different now, at tiny scale.** (1) A strong teacher exists and can be asked *once per word type* for a root/concept proposal, which turns the historically impossible task (hand-writing the interlingua lexicon) into a verification problem MIZAN already knows how to run (Tathabbut, Isnad two-witness rule, Sakk receipts). (2) The interlingua does not need to be complete or a bottleneck: as a *side channel* beside bytes, an unmapped word costs nothing (it falls back to bytes) and a wrongly mapped word is caught by the verifiers. (3) Open concept pivots now exist with compatible licences: Concepticon (CC BY 4.0), Arabic WordNet 4.0 (CC BY 4.0, March 2026), Wikidata (CC0), while BabelNet and CLE Urdu WordNet are non-commercial and should be avoided as training sources.
- **Alignment must be seeded, never unsupervised.** Urdu, Hindi and Arabic are morphologically rich and Urdu/Hinglish corpora differ in domain from Wikipedia — exactly the two conditions under which Søgaard found P@1 collapse to 0.00. But the seed MIZAN can get for free is unusually strong: Urdu and Arabic share thousands of **identically spelled** Perso-Arabic loanwords after letter normalisation (ʿilm, kitāb, waqt …), Hinglish and English share identical Latin strings, and all five languages share digits — precisely the "identical strings" weak supervision that lifted en-fi from 0.09 to 28.01. The teacher then adds a few thousand verified pairs (Procrustes with a 5k dictionary beat unsupervised on every distant pair in Conneau's table: en-ru 51.7 vs 44.0, en-zh 42.7 vs 32.5).
- **Expected BLI numbers (inference).** No published P@1 for en-ur, en-hi, hi-ur or ur-ar was found. By analogy with en-ru (51.7 supervised, Cyrillic, morphologically rich) and en-zh (42.7), and given smaller Urdu Wikipedia (507k sentences in Dakshina's extract vs 1,065k for Hindi), I expect **P@1 ≈ 30–45% for en-hi and en-ur with a 5k seed on Wikipedia embeddings**, higher (50–65%) for **ur-ar restricted to Perso-Arabic loan vocabulary** because of orthographic identity, and lower (<30%) for Roman Urdu until spelling is normalised. These are targets to measure, not results.
- **D9 of the earlier report is invalidated by the zero-spend rule** (it assumed rented H100s at $1.99/h for LoRA students). Its licence filter, data hygiene and McNemar/e-value promotion gates survive and apply unchanged to the lexicon-induction loop below; the "student" becomes the factored CPU model, not an 8B LoRA.

### Gaps
- No evaluation of UNL-based MT quality or an account of why UNL was not adopted was found; only the system description.
- SONAR's parameter count is not stated in the paper or model card (24+24 layers initialised from NLLB-1B implies on the order of 1B parameters — inference); the model is CC-BY-NC and far beyond 2 GB RAM in fp32, so it was not examined further as a runtime component.
- No published bilingual-lexicon-induction results for en-ur, en-hi, hi-ur or ur-ar were found.
- Concepticon's current concept-set count is not shown on its landing page; only historical figures (~3,755) are cited.

## Key Question 3 — Arabic, Urdu, Hindi and Roman-script resources and tools: what exists, under which licences, and how good is it?

### Takeaway
Licence-clean building blocks exist on the Arabic side (CAMeL Tools code MIT, its default MSA morphology database r13 GPL v2 at 40.5 MB; Arabic WordNet 4.0 CC BY 4.0; the Quranic Arabic Corpus GPL with a non-commercial clause), on the Indic side (IndicXlit models MIT and data CC-BY, 26M transliteration pairs; Dakshina CC BY-SA 4.0 with 25k-word romanisation lexica for Hindi and Urdu), and for Hinglish (L3Cube-HingCorpus 1.04B tokens). MADAMIRA and Farasa are research/non-commercial. Roman Urdu → Urdu transliteration is the weakest link: the Dakshina baselines reach 63.3% word error on isolated Urdu words (19.5% CER) and 12.5–18.9% WER in sentence context.

### Cited Findings

**Arabic morphology**
- Quranic Arabic Corpus (Kais Dukes, Leeds): data "available under the GNU public license"; the FAQ's terms add "You do not use the data for commercial purposes" and "You give a citation in your research publication" — [corpus.quran.com FAQ](https://Corpus.Quran.com/faq.jsp); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quranic_Arabic_Corpus); downstream reusers must "clearly indicate the Quranic Arabic Corpus as the source with a link" — [Quranpedia LICENSE](https://quranpedia.net/dumps/LICENSE.md). No results were found for UrduHack specifically.

**Romanisation / transliteration (Roman Urdu, Hinglish)**

- Aksharantar / IndicXlit (AI4Bharat 2022): ~26M romanised-to-native pairs, 21 languages, 12 scripts, "roughly 21 times larger" than prior sets; 103k-pair test set over 19 languages; IndicXlit gives a 15% accuracy gain on Dakshina and SOTA on all 12 shared languages; earlier top-1 accuracies were 33.2–67.6% (avg 51.8%); models MIT, data CC-BY — [arXiv 2205.03018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.03018)
- Roman Urdu lexical normalisation: unsupervised clustering with UrduPhone phonetic encoding and Lex-Var k-medoids over four SMS/blog datasets, F-measure gain up to 15% over baselines; code on GitHub — [Khan et al., arXiv 2004.00088 / Natural Language Engineering 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00088)
- English→Roman Urdu parallel data: ERUPD, 75,146 sentence pairs (2024) — surfaced in the same search; Hinglish NLI/QA in GLUECoS uses Romanised Hindi and follow-up work transliterated with `indic-trans` — [GLUECoS summary](https://liner.com/review/gluecos-evaluation-benchmark-for-codeswitched-nlp)

### Inferences
- **Licence map for MIZAN.** Safe to ship or train on: CAMeL Tools code (MIT) + r13 database (GPL v2: usable server-side as data; keep it a downloaded resource like QAC, never vendored into MIT code), Arabic WordNet 4.0 (CC BY 4.0), Concepticon (CC BY 4.0), Wikidata (CC0), Dakshina (CC BY-SA 4.0: use only for *evaluation* and for a non-shipped lexicon, as the earlier report did with FLORES+), IndicXlit (MIT models, CC-BY data), fastText-style vectors trained by MIZAN on open text. Not safe: MADAMIRA, Farasa, BabelNet, CLE Urdu WordNet (non-commercial/research terms), the QAC *for commercial purposes* (MIZAN is a personal self-hosted agent; the owner should still record the citation requirement in `LICENSES.md`, which `backend/tadbir/data/lughah/LICENSES.md` already models). IndoWordNet's licence is disputed between sources; verify before use.
- **Analyzer choice.** The 1,651-root Quranic table is the *vocabulary of the Quran*, not of Arabic; for root ids over modern Arabic and over Perso-Arabic loans, CAMeL's r13 database (40.5 MB, GPL v2, pure Python, CPU) is the practical analyzer, with the Quranic root ids as a subset so that Quranic grounding stays exact. MIZAN's own analyzer at 47% root accuracy is not good enough to label training data; the recent LREC 2026 finding that morphological tokenizer alignment is "neither necessary nor sufficient" for generation also warns against expecting the root factorisation to help *generation* by itself — its value is in *alignment and retrieval* across languages, which is what the interlingua is for.
- **Roman Urdu should map to roots at the word level, not via full transliteration.** With 63.3% WER on isolated Urdu words and 4.2 attested romanisations per type, a Roman→Urdu→root pipeline would lose most roots. Instead, learn a direct **Roman-spelling → (root, concept)** lexicon: cluster spelling variants with UrduPhone-style phonetic keys (Khan et al.), ask the teacher once per *cluster*, verify by (a) transliteration agreement with IndicXlit and (b) Dakshina's attested romanisations where the word is in its lexicon. Hinglish English tokens map through the English concept lexicon unchanged.
- **Hindi.** The same Perso-Arabic loan set appears in Devanagari (वक़्त, इल्म, किताब); IndicNLP-style normalisation (nukta dropping) plus the Hindi–Urdu transliteration lexicon from Dakshina/IndicXlit gives the Hindi side of the loan vocabulary nearly for free, and IndoWordNet's Hindi (38,607) and Urdu (34,280) synsets are already linked by construction, which is the cleanest Hindi↔Urdu concept pivot available.

### Gaps
- Per-language Hindi/Urdu accuracies for IndicXlit on Dakshina were not extracted (only the "15% gain on Dakshina" and the 33.2–67.6% prior-work range).
- No Hinglish-specific *normalisation* dataset was found (only Roman Urdu normalisation and general transliteration sets).
- Farasa's and MADAMIRA's full licence texts were not retrieved; the terms above come from their official pages' summaries.
- IndoWordNet's licence (GNU per Wikipedia vs the LDC agreement for the 2008 release) could not be settled.

## Key Question 4 — How to BUILD the mapping on 2 vCPU / 2 GB with the teacher used once

### Takeaway
A lexicon-induction loop is feasible on the production CPU: fastText-style subword skip-gram runs at ~105k words/s/thread, so 100M tokens × 5 epochs on 2 threads is on the order of 40 minutes of compute if the paper's hardware is matched (plan for 1.5–3 h on a shared vCPU), provided the vector table is shrunk to fit 2 GB (e.g. 100 dimensions and ≤400k rows, ~160 MB, instead of fastText's default 2M hash buckets at 300-d, which alone would be ~2.6 GB). Alignment is orthogonal Procrustes seeded with identical strings, digits and the teacher's verified pairs, then refined with CSLS mutual nearest neighbours; every lexicon entry is a verified, receipted record with provenance.

### Cited Findings
- fastText subword model: "we process 105k words/second/thread … versus 145k words/second/thread for the baseline"; 300-d vectors, five passes, character n-grams of length 3–6, hashing into K = 2·10⁶ buckets — [Bojanowski et al., arXiv 1607.04606](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1607.04606); the classifier variant trains "on a billion words within ten minutes" on a multicore CPU with 20 threads — [Joulin et al., arXiv 1607.01759](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/1607.01759); a 2021 re-measurement: subword skip-gram on the 2017 English Wikipedia dump (14.3 GiB), one epoch, about 1 h 11 min — [arXiv 2104.09691](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.09691); defaults: 12 threads, 100 dimensions, 5 epochs, minn 3 / maxn 6 — [fastText unsupervised tutorial](https://fasttext.cc/docs/en/unsupervised-tutorial.html)
- Orthogonal Procrustes: with X source and Y target embeddings of a seed dictionary, W* = argmin_{W∈O_d} ‖WX − Y‖_F = UVᵀ where UΣVᵀ = SVD(YXᵀ); CSLS retrieval score 2cos(Wx, y) − r_T(Wx) − r_S(y) with r the mean cosine to the k = 10 nearest neighbours in the other language; refinement iterates Procrustes on mutual nearest neighbours among the 15k most frequent words — [Conneau et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1710.04087); supervised Procrustes with 5k pairs: en-ru 51.7, en-zh 42.7 P@1 (same source)
- Seeds that work when unsupervised fails: identical strings (en-fi 28.01 vs 0.09) — [Søgaard et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1805.03620); 25 word pairs (EN-FI 28.16) or numerals only (26.47), and robust self-learning from a weak start (EN-FI 32.63, 10/10 runs) — [Artetxe et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1805.06297)
- CPU training budget for the Ruh architecture on 2 torch threads: tiny 1.03M-param model 3,954 train tok/s (85M tokens/day at 25% duty), small 5.43M 1,809 tok/s, mid 15.39M 1,034 tok/s, default 45.99M 574 tok/s; one 1,024-token gradient step costs 0.26–1.8 s; weights + Adam state ≤200 MB for ≤50M params — sibling [E1](/research/notes/MIZAN own model and living memory/experiments_cpu_and_root_coverage.md)
- Naive Bayes needs O(log n) samples in the number of features against O(n) for logistic regression, and "may approach its (higher) asymptotic error much faster" — [Ng & Jordan 2001](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2001/hash/7b7a53e239400a13bd6be6c91c4f6c4e-Abstract.html); [Zheng et al., ICML 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.02334) (as cited in the earlier report)
- Open text sources with sizes relevant to CPU embedding training: Dakshina native-script Wikipedia hi 1,065k / ur 507.3k sentences at ~25 words each (≈26M / ≈13M words) — [Dakshina](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.01176v1); L3Cube-HingCorpus 1.04B Roman-Hinglish tokens — [arXiv 2204.08398](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08398); MASSIVE 1M utterances over 51 locales (hi-IN train/dev/test 11,514/2,033/2,974) — [HF dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/AmazonScience/massive)

### Inferences (the design: "Qāmūs", a verified cross-lingual root/concept lexicon; mathematics explicit)

1. **Representation.** Each word type w in language L gets a record `(w, L, concept c ∈ C, root r ∈ R ∪ {∅}, pattern p ∈ P ∪ {∅}, sense tag, provenance, strength)`. C is seeded from open inventories: Concepticon concept sets for core vocabulary (the 65 NSM primes and the Swadesh/Leipzig-Jakarta lists are all Concepticon-linked), Arabic WordNet 4.0 synset ids for Arabic and its loan vocabulary, IndoWordNet ids for Hindi/Urdu, Wikidata lexeme/sense ids for named concepts. R is the CAMeL r13 root inventory with the 1,651 Quranic roots marked (so a `quran_search`/Sarf answer can still be grounded in the Quranic Arabic Corpus exactly). The Ruh/Bayan `(root_id, pattern_id)` pair becomes two optional slots beside a byte sequence, not the vocabulary; the sibling audit already recommends exactly this ("keep as an auxiliary channel added to a byte or BPE embedding").

2. **Monolingual vectors on CPU.** Train subword skip-gram vectors per language (ur, hi, hi-Latn/ur-Latn, en, ar) on open text. Budget: at 105k words/s/thread and 2 threads, T = (tokens × epochs)/(2 × 105k). For 100M tokens × 5 epochs: T ≈ 2,380 s ≈ 40 min if the production vCPUs match the paper's cores; plan for 1.5–3 h in the background (shared vCPU, Python overhead), still "hours", as the task assumed. Memory is the binding constraint, not time: fastText's default 2M buckets × 300 d × 4 B ≈ 2.4 GB plus 200k words × 300 × 4 ≈ 240 MB exceeds the 2 GB container. Use d = 100, vocabulary ≤200k, buckets ≤200k: (200k + 200k) × 100 × 4 B ≈ 160 MB per language, 0.8 GB for five, or train one language at a time and keep only the word table (80 MB each). Hinglish gets its own space (HingCorpus is 1.04B tokens; 100M is plenty, cf. Søgaard's warning that domain mismatch kills alignment, so the Hinglish space should be trained on chat-like text, not Wikipedia).

3. **Seed dictionary without the teacher.** D₀ = identical strings after normalisation: digits; Latin tokens shared by en and hi-Latn/ur-Latn (same spelling); Perso-Arabic loans spelled identically in ur and ar after mapping Urdu letters (ہ→ه, ی→ي, ک→ك, ے→ي, ۃ→ة) and stripping diacritics; Hindi–Urdu pairs from Dakshina/IndicXlit transliteration (evaluation-only for Dakshina). This is the Smith/Søgaard "identical strings" seed that lifted en-fi from 0.09 to 28.01 and the Artetxe numerals heuristic (26.47 EN-FI from numbers alone).

4. **Alignment.** For each pair (L, pivot) solve W* = UVᵀ with UΣVᵀ = SVD(Y Xᵀ) over D_t, retrieve with CSLS, keep mutual nearest neighbours among the top-15k words as D_{t+1}, iterate to convergence (Conneau's refinement; Artetxe's self-learning with stochastic dictionary dropout if D₀ is small). Pivot: English for en/hi/hi-Latn and Arabic for ar/ur (then compose), because the shared-string seed is densest on those edges. Expected P@1 after seeding (inference, KQ2): 30–45% en-hi/en-ur, 50–65% ur-ar on loan vocabulary, <30% for Roman Urdu before normalisation.

5. **The teacher, once per word type, by frequency.** Process word types in descending corpus frequency f(w) so coverage by token rises fastest: coverage after the first k types is Σ_{i≤k} f(w_i)/Σ f (Zipf: the top ~5k types typically cover 80–90% of tokens in chat). For each unresolved w the teacher is asked one structured question: gloss, concept candidates from the open inventories, Arabic root (or NONE, with the reason: Indo-Aryan, Persian, English, name), pattern, and whether the Urdu/Hindi sense differs from the Arabic root sense (the *mazdūr/gharīb* problem). The request is a `use_tool`-style schema call; MIZAN checks the JSON (Fahm/Lisan already do this), and the model never writes into the lexicon directly.

6. **Verification: three independent witnesses, Isnad grading.** (a) *Dictionary witness*: the proposed root/concept exists in CAMeL r13 / Arabic WordNet 4.0 / IndoWordNet / Wiktionary and the proposed gloss overlaps the entry's gloss (BM25 or IDF-weighted overlap above the word's mean, the rule Ahl al-Dhikr already uses). (b) *Embedding witness*: the aligned vector of w has the proposed concept's exponent in the pivot language within its CSLS top-k (k = 5), i.e. rank_CSLS(Wx_w, y_c) ≤ 5. (c) *Back-translation witness*: a separate teacher call, without seeing the first, is given the concept gloss and asked for the L word; the answer must be w or share w's root/stem (Tathabbut's "independent program" rule applied to words). Grade like Isnad: three agree → sahih (kept, strength 1.0); two → hasan (kept, strength 0.7, re-checked on first use); one → da'if (not kept; queued for the admin via Ta'lim); a dictionary contradiction (root exists but with an unrelated gloss) → mawdu' (rejected, and the teacher's proposal logged as a Tajriba lesson). Probability of a wrong entry surviving when witnesses are independent with error rates e_a, e_b, e_c: ≈ e_a e_b + e_a e_c + e_b e_c; with e ≈ 0.15 each, ≈ 6.8% — so a per-user correction path (Mahw-erasable) must remain.

7. **Strength and forgetting.** Each entry carries the same FSRS-6 strength Tadhkir already gives memories: every verified use (a Fahm plan that executed and verified with this entry in the path) is a Good review; an entry that causes a Sidq correction or a user correction is downgraded and, below threshold, re-asked once. This makes the lexicon self-correcting with no model call in the steady state and gives the "learning by itself" property the owner asks for, with a formula already in the codebase.

8. **Cost of the teacher.** One structured call per cluster of word types, ~5–10k calls total for 80–90% token coverage per language (Zipf), each a few hundred tokens: tens of thousands of tokens per language, a one-time cost of a few US dollars at the OpenRouter prices the earlier report cites, then zero. Entries learned from a model whose terms permit training (DeepSeek, open weights) may seed the small model's embedding table; entries from Anthropic/OpenAI models may be used as *lexicon data MIZAN looks up* but not as training targets — the earlier report's licence filter applies per entry via `provenance.model`.

9. **How the factored small model consumes it.** Input token = bytes of the word (UTF-8, as Ruh v2 already does, lossless) plus three additive embeddings looked up from Qāmūs when present: E_concept[c] + E_root[r] + E_pattern[p]; unknown words get only bytes. With |C| ≈ 20k, |R| ≈ 6k, |P| ≈ 200 at d = 128 the tables add (20k+6k+0.2k)×128×4 B ≈ 13 MB. The concept table is **shared across the five languages**, which is the interlingua; the model never needs a bottleneck. Training signal: the same supervised bytes Ruh v2 uses, plus a contrastive term pulling words of the same concept in different languages together (InfoNCE over aligned pairs from Qāmūs). E1 shows a 5–15M model trains to 20 tokens/parameter in days to two weeks at 25% duty, so adding ~13 MB of tables is affordable.

### Gaps
- No independent CPU wall-clock measurement of fastText skip-gram on 2 threads was found; the 40-minute estimate is derived from the paper's per-thread throughput and must be measured on the production container (the E1 script can be extended).
- No published result on seeding Procrustes with *Perso-Arabic shared spellings* between Urdu and Arabic was found; the expected 50–65% P@1 is inference.
- The Zipf coverage claim ("top 5k types ≈ 80–90% of chat tokens") is a general rule of thumb; it should be computed on MIZAN's own corpora (Lughah seeds, HingCorpus sample) before committing the teacher budget.

## Key Question 5 — Evaluation plan: benchmarks, baselines and expected numbers

### Takeaway
Small-scale benchmarks exist for all five languages (MASSIVE intents ur-PK/hi-IN/ar-SA/en-US, XNLI/IndicXNLI, LinCE and GLUECoS for Hinglish, Dakshina for romanisation, FLORES-200 for translation). The published reference points are XLM-R-class models (270–550M parameters): MASSIVE full-data intent accuracy hi 85.8 / ur 83.2 / ar 80.7 / en 88.3, zero-shot from English hi 74.8 / ur 65.6 / ar 62.8; XNLI translate-train-all ar 83.1 / hi 81.6 / ur 78.1. A 5–15M CPU model cannot reach these; the honest test is a **paired comparison against an equal-size byte/char baseline** (MIZAN's own Tamyiz and a byte-only Ruh v2), scored with the McNemar/e-value gates already in the codebase.

### Cited Findings
- MASSIVE (FitzGerald et al. 2022): 1M utterances, 51 languages, 18 domains, 60 intents, 55 slots; XLM-R Base full-data intent accuracy / slot F1 / exact match: en-US 88.3 / 78.7 / 69.7; **hi-IN 85.8 / 72.3 / 63.4; ur-PK 83.2 / 68.0 / 59.0; ar-SA 80.7 / 73.8 / 61.2**; zero-shot (English-only training): hi-IN 74.8 / 54.6 / 42.6; ur-PK 65.6 / 41.4 / 30.5; ar-SA 62.8 / 48.4 / 31.2; all-locale averages full 85.1 / 73.6 / 63.7, zero-shot 70.6 / 50.3 / 38.7; mT5 full 85.3 / 76.8 / 66.6 — [arXiv 2204.08582](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.08582)
- XNLI accuracy (Conneau et al. 2020): XLM-R (550M) translate-train-all en 89.1, **ar 83.1, hi 81.6, ur 78.1**, avg 83.6; cross-lingual transfer ar 79.8, hi 76.9, ur 73.8, avg 80.9; mBERT cross-lingual ar 64.9, hi 60.0, ur 58.0, avg 66.3; XLM-R Base 270M parameters — [arXiv 1911.02116](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1911.02116)

- Byte-level vs subword: ByT5 beat mT5 at matched parameter counts on in-language XTREME tasks; the per-language gap tracks subword compression, and the ByT5 paper measured stability "across languages" at Large size — [ByT5, arXiv 2105.13626](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.13626); ByT5 produced higher-quality MT than mT5 "especially when resources are limited" — [Edman et al., TACL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.22.pdf)
- Cross-lingual transfer alone loses ~15 points on MASSIVE for ur/ta (83.2/83.5 → 65.6/68.1) and in-language data closes the gap — earlier report, section "Transfer alone loses about fifteen points" ([`research/reports/MIZAN self learning architecture.md`](/research/reports/MIZAN%20self%20learning%20architecture.md))
- Paired-test sizing: detecting +3 points with McNemar at one-sided α = 0.05, power 0.8 needs ≈ 686 items; Tajdid's anytime-valid e-value gate (α_k = 0.05·2^−k, Ville's inequality) is already implemented — earlier report D7/D9 and `core/adaptive.paired_e_value`

### Inferences (the plan)
- **Primary question to answer, with controls.** Does adding the root/concept channel to an equal-size byte/char model improve understanding of Urdu, Hinglish and Arabic at MIZAN's scale? Three arms, identical parameter counts (±2%) and identical training tokens: (A) byte-only Ruh v2 (5–15M params), (B) A + root/pattern slots from Qāmūs, (C) A + concept + root/pattern slots. A fourth, non-neural arm is MIZAN's Tamyiz (hashed char n-gram naive Bayes), which is the real production baseline and, per Ng & Jordan, the strongest contender at tens of examples per class.
- **Tasks and sets.** (1) Intent: MASSIVE ur-PK, hi-IN, ar-SA, en-US (official splits) plus MIZAN's Qasd seeds under leave-one-out; (2) NLI: XNLI ur/ar/hi test and IndicXNLI hi; (3) Hinglish: LinCE sentiment and GLUECoS NLI; (4) Bilingual lexicon induction P@1 on a held-out slice of Qāmūs (sahih entries only) and on MUSE's published en-hi/en-ur/en-ar dictionaries where available; (5) Root recall: share of tokens in Lughah ur/ar and MASSIVE ur/ar given a root, versus CAMeL r13 as reference for Arabic; (6) romanisation robustness: accuracy on Dakshina ur sentences romanised vs native.
- **Expected numbers (inference).** XLM-R's 83.2 (ur) / 85.8 (hi) / 80.7 (ar) MASSIVE intent accuracies are the ceiling a 270M model reaches with 11.5k in-language examples; a 5–15M byte model trained on CPU should be expected in the 60–75% range in-language (the zero-shot XLM-R numbers of 62.8–74.8 are a reasonable proxy for "weak model"), and Tamyiz in a similar band on 60 intents with hashed n-grams. The root/concept channel should help most where (i) data per class is small, (ii) the language is Urdu/Arabic (highest root coverage) and (iii) requests are religious/abstract — I expect **+2 to +6 points intent accuracy on ur-PK and ar-SA in the low-data regime (≤100 examples per intent), ≤+2 on hi-IN, ≈0 on en-US and on Hinglish overall**, and a larger effect on **cross-lingual transfer** (train ur, test ar and vice versa), where shared concept ids are the only bridge a byte model lacks. If the full-data hi-IN/en-US gain is not ≈0, suspect leakage through the lexicon.
- **Decision rule.** Promote the channel only if the paired e-value against arm A crosses 1/α_k on ≥686 held-out items per language (MASSIVE test sets have 2,974 per locale, enough), and non-regression holds on en-US and on Hinglish (LinCE sentiment), exactly the two-gate structure of the earlier report's D9. Report Wilson intervals as Imtihan already does.
- **What would falsify the interlingua idea.** If arm C does not beat arm A on ur→ar transfer with a sahih lexicon covering ≥20% of tokens, the shared concept slot is not carrying meaning across languages at this scale and the root space should be kept only for Sarf, Fihris and Quranic grounding (its already-verified uses), with the byte model learning cross-lingual structure from data alone.
- **Measurement MIZAN must add first** (sibling E3 found it missing): the share of answers with zero hosted-model calls, per language, so that the lexicon's effect on teacher dependence is visible as the loop runs.

### Gaps
- No published small-model (≤50M parameters) results on MASSIVE ur/hi/ar, XNLI ur or LinCE were found; the 60–75% band and the +2 to +6 point expectations are inference from the XLM-R zero-shot numbers and must be measured.
- No clean ByT5-small vs mT5-small comparison on low-resource languages was found; the byte-vs-subword evidence is at Base/Large size.
- FLORES-200 translation was not pursued because generation is out of scope for a 5–15M model (E1: ~13 bytes/s without a KV cache); it remains the right set for evaluating the *lexicon's* word-level coverage of translation references.
- MUSE's published ground-truth dictionaries for en-hi/en-ur/en-ar exist in the MUSE repository but their sizes and quality for these pairs were not verified here.
